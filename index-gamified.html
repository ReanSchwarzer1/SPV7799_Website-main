1. The Molecular Scrollyteller: Imatinib (Gleevec)

The Synthesis Puzzle

The chemical formula for imatinib (sold by Novartis as Gleevec in the US and Glivec elsewhere) is C29H31N7O. It is used for treatment of chronic myelogenous leukemia and gastrointestinal stromal tumours. It has a role as an antineoplastic agent, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor and an apoptosis inducer.

After its US launch in 2001, Novartis raised the US price several times. In 2013 the Supreme Court of India refused Novartis a patent on a new crystalline form of the drug under Section 3(d), so Indian manufacturers kept selling generic versions.

2. The Pipeline: Why Patents Exist

Before a generic can copy a medicine, someone has to invent it. Most candidate cancer drugs fail in clinical trials, and the firm pays for those failures out of the few drugs that reach patients. A patent gives the inventor a period in which rivals cannot copy the drug, so that money can be recovered. Set how many candidates enter trials and how long the winner keeps the market before generics arrive.

Sources, Formula and the Counter-Case
1. Attrition and cost
  • 3.4% of oncology development programmes in clinical trials reached approval, in data on trials from 2000 to 2015 (Wong, Siah & Lo, 2019).
  • Median capitalized R&D investment per approved drug: $1,141.7M across 63 drugs approved 2009–2018, and $2,771.6M for antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (2018 US dollars; Wouters, McKee & Luyten, 2020, as corrected in 2022). These figures include failed programmes and a 10.5% yearly cost of capital.
  • An industry-data estimate puts the capitalized pre-approval cost at $2,558M per approved compound (2013 dollars; DiMasi, Grabowski & Hansen, 2016).
2. The recovery formula
R = C × r / (1 − (1 + r)−T)
  • R is the constant net revenue per year of exclusivity whose present value equals C, the $2,771.6M capitalized cost, at r = 10.5%. T is the years of exclusivity.
  • Illustrative: it ignores the years sales take to build, sales after generic entry, and tax.
  • The WTO's TRIPS agreement (Article 33) sets a patent term of at least 20 years from the filing date. Firms usually file before trials, so the years of protected sales are fewer than 20.
3. The counter-case, and its limits
  • If generics arrive before the cost is recovered, fewer drugs are worth developing. High-income markets can pay prices above marginal cost that fund the shared R&D, while lower prices in poorer markets widen access (Danzon & Towse, 2003).
  • One global price would not fix this. When low prices spill over through reference pricing and parallel trade, firms delay or skip launches in low-price markets: only about 55% of potential launches of 85 new drugs happened across 25 markets (Danzon, Wang & Wang, 2005).
  • The limit: patent protection was associated with more R&D in high-income countries, but introducing patents in developing countries was not followed by more investment in diseases that mainly affect the poorest countries (Kyle & McGahan, 2012).

4. Who pays, and how
  • India: out-of-pocket spending was 39.4% of total health expenditure in 2021–22, and government spending 48% (National Health Accounts, MoHFW, 2024). Many patients pay the pharmacy price themselves, which is why section 7 counts price in days of wages.
  • United States: list prices are cut by confidential rebates and discounts negotiated with payers; these gross-to-net reductions on brand-name drugs were estimated at $335 billion in 2023 (Drug Channels Institute, 2024). Medicare's first negotiated prices for 10 drugs, 38% to 79% below list, take effect on 1 January 2026 (Cooley, 2024).
  • So a US list price overstates what insurers pay, while an Indian pharmacy price is close to what a patient pays.

Why QALYs Decide Coverage
1. The coverage rule
ICER = ΔCost / ΔQALYs ≤ λ
fund the treatment if its cost per QALY gained is at or below the threshold λ
  • With a fixed budget, money spent on one treatment is not spent on another. The threshold λ is the health that money would have bought elsewhere: the cost per QALY of what the budget gives up (Claxton et al., 2015; McCabe, Claxton & Culyer, 2008).
  • The budget is spent best when the last QALY it buys costs λ: the marginal benefit of spending equals its marginal cost. A treatment above λ displaces more health than it adds.
2. Reference points
  • United States, 2004 prices: imatinib against interferon-alpha plus low-dose cytarabine gained 3.89 QALYs per patient at $43,300 per QALY (Reed et al., 2004).
  • India has no official threshold. A 2026 survey estimated willingness to pay per QALY at INR 2,12,307 (US$2,535), 1 to 1.52 times GDP per capita (Chugh et al., 2026).
  • WHO authors advise against using multiples of GDP per capita as decision rules on their own (Bertram et al., 2016). The US debate is over a $50,000 benchmark (Neumann et al., 2014).
3. What this calculator does and does not do

In the IRIS trial, estimated overall survival on imatinib was 83.3% at 10 years (Hochhaus et al., 2017). A published cost-effectiveness model reports 7.34 QALYs per patient over 10 years for starting on imatinib (Yamamoto et al., 2019).

The bars count QALYs lived by treated patients, not QALYs gained over a comparator, so they are not an ICER. An Indian ICER would need Indian costs and outcomes for imatinib and its comparator, which are not modelled here.

The Policy Math & Economic Theory
1. The S-Shaped (Logistic) Diffusion Curve
dnt = β (nt / N) (N - nt) dt
  • Where nt is the number of adopters and N is the total population.
  • Adoption starts slowly, speeds up as each adopter makes the next adoption easier, then slows as few non-adopters remain. The solution of this equation is a logistic, S-shaped curve.
  • The curve drawn here is illustrative: its midpoint (2010) and slope (0.4) were chosen, not fitted to the export figures.
2. Complementary Assets
  • Diffusion requires "complementary assets": organizational change, infrastructure, and capabilities.
  • For Indian generics, low prices were not enough on their own; buyers also needed a way to certify and pay for them. After the US FDA set up a tentative-approval route for generic antiretrovirals bought with PEPFAR funds, the generic share of antiretroviral packs PEPFAR procured rose from 14.8% in 2005 to 89.3% in 2008, saving an estimated $323M; 9 of the 12 manufacturers with tentative approval were based in India (Holmes et al., 2010).

The Policy Math & Economic Theory
1. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index
H = Σ Si2
  • The index calculates concentration by adding up the squares of every firm's market share percentage.
  • Squaring the shares severely penalizes markets dominated by a single giant.
  • < 1,500: Unconcentrated
  • 1,500 - 2,500: Moderate
  • > 2,500: Monopoly Threat
2. The Innovation Debate
  • Arrow (1962) proposed that monopolists lack the drive to innovate because new products simply cannibalize their existing sales (the replacement effect).
  • Schumpeter (1942) countered that only massive monopolies possess the financial capital and economies of scale required to survive high-risk R&D failures.
3. The Inverted-U Relationship
  • Aghion et al. (2005) merged these theories into an inverted-U model.
  • In moderately competitive markets, closely matched firms invest heavily in R&D to break ahead of the pack.
  • In hyper-competitive markets, prices are driven down so close to marginal cost that no firm retains enough profit to fund research.

↳ Source: Aghion, P., et al. (2005). "Competition and Innovation: an Inverted-U Relationship." The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(2), 701–728.

9. Game Theory: The Patent Race, and What Happens When the Prize Shrinks

Two firms race for one patent. The bigger spender wins it; equal spenders split it on a coin toss. With a large prize both firms overspend. But the prize depends on how long the winner keeps the market before generics enter. Shorten that period and watch the equilibrium move, from a wasteful race to the drug not being developed at all.

The Policy Math & Sensitivity Analysis
1. Payoffs
  • Win outright (outspend the rival, or invest alone): V − C.
  • Tie (equal spending): V/2 − C, the expected value of a coin toss.
  • Lose: −C. Stay out: 0. High costs $400M, Low $100M.
2. How the prize shrinks
V(T) = 1000 × (1 − 1.105−T) / (1 − 1.105−20)
  • Same yearly profit, fewer years before generic entry, discounted at the 10.5% cost of capital from section 2. V(20) = $1,000M.
  • High beats Low against either rival choice only when V > 2 × (400 − 100) = $600M.
3. What the stages show
  • 20 years ($1,000M): both go High and net $100M each, against $400M each if both held back. Competition in R&D pushes effort past what is socially worthwhile (Dasgupta & Stiglitz, 1980).
  • 10 years ($731M): no stable pair of choices.
  • 5 years ($455M): both go Low; the race stops.
  • 1 year ($110M): only one firm can invest profitably.
  • 6 months ($56M): nobody invests, so the drug is not developed. The $1,000M, $400M and $100M are illustrative; the pattern is the point.

10. Global Benchmarks: The Generic Paradox, and Beyond the OECD

Data from OECD Health at a Glance 2023 show a "generic paradox." In Germany, generics made up 83% of the reimbursed market by volume in 2021 but only 29% of its value; across 26 OECD countries the averages were 54% and 25%. Generics carry most of the volume at a small share of the bill.
